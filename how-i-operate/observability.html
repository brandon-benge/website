Observability and SRE Leadership at Enterprise Scale

A cross-project view of how I used telemetry across GCP, Azure, Kubernetes, and OpenStack-backed infrastructure to manage reliability, cost, incidents, customer impact, and engineering capacity.

The leadership capability

As Senior Manager for Walmart’s Streaming Engines organization, I was accountable for the operational health of a cloud-native and hybrid portfolio that grew to more than 3,500 workloads. Its public-cloud footprint included roughly 70 Kubernetes clusters—approximately 35 in GKE and 35 in Azure—while OpenStack supported Kubernetes capacity on bare-metal infrastructure. Together, these environments represented approximately $26 million in annual infrastructure.

For five years, I served as the team’s subject-matter expert for operational dashboards, alerting, golden signals, and runtime-data analysis. I wrote PromQL, built and reviewed Grafana views, examined workload and infrastructure behavior, and helped decide which signals should page an engineer, inform a service-level objective, or become an input to capacity and roadmap decisions.

The full observability path

The operating model connected metrics, logs, traces, profiles, tickets, and incident reviews rather than treating them as separate tools. Prometheus collected platform and workload metrics close to Kubernetes clusters. Mimir supplied a global metrics layer; Grafana and PromQL supported fleet, capacity, adoption, and cost views; and Splunk supported centralized log search.

GKE Cloud Logging and Cloud Monitoring supplied Google Cloud system, audit, application-log, and system-metric context. Azure Log Analytics and Application Insights provided corresponding Azure views. Fluent Bit, node exporter, kube-state-metrics, JMX exporters, Kafka Connect and Spark/Flink telemetry, Coroot, eBPF signals, and JVM profiling enabled investigation from fleet health down to a single workload.

Local resilience with global visibility

Local Prometheus collection preserved detection close to workloads when a network or global dependency was impaired. Central aggregation enabled fleet-wide rules and governance across GCP, Azure, and OpenStack-backed environments. Grafana links carried cluster, namespace, pod, application, connector, tenant, and time-range context directly into Splunk searches, reducing the work required to reconstruct an investigation.

DORA in context: delivery decisions and outcomes

My streaming-platform work connected delivery speed and operational stability to the experience of the teams using the platform. The examples below show the problems I prioritized, the decisions I made with partners, and the outcomes we recorded.

A shared deployment path for streaming teams

Problem: Tenant feedback and support demand exposed friction in deployment and compliance. Policies for disaster recovery (DR), job health monitoring, and availability required a consistent way to define, monitor, and restore streaming workloads across clusters.

Decision: My technical lead and I co-owned the initial managed-ArgoCD strategy. We used GitOps as the source of truth (SoT), reconciliation to bring running workloads back into alignment with that declared state, and cluster abstraction to give tenants a consistent deployment path across clusters. These capabilities responded directly to compliance policies for DR, job health monitoring, and availability. I carried the program through completion after my technical lead left, aligning Security, Kubernetes, and Dev Tools around deployment templates and governance requirements.

Outcome: A consistent, monitored deployment path increased platform users’ trust and gave teams the confidence to deploy more frequently with low change-failure risk. Managed ArgoCD reached 100% adoption across the streaming portfolio. First-time deployments took 15 minutes or less, the platform supported hundreds of deployments per hour, and it monitored thousands of updates a day. Adoption grew from zero to more than 3,500 streams in just over five years. That growth reflected users’ willingness to put more streaming workloads on a platform where frequent changes could be made safely.

Recovery objectives were below 30 minutes RTO and five minutes RPO. The model expanded into a supported Dev Tools offering beyond Data Platform. For platform users, deployment frequency and change failure rate were connected to trust: being able to release more often with low failure risk helped make continued adoption possible.

Because this was an early platform decision, its individual impact on deployment frequency and adoption was difficult to isolate. The thousands of monitored daily updates describe platform activity, rather than a count of distinct production releases; no before-and-after deployment-frequency or change-failure-rate comparison is claimed here. Resetting the roadmap when reliability was at risk

Problem: FYE2023 was a critical year of headcount reduction: the organization contracted from approximately 14 contributors to four engineers. Alert load, operational demand, and burnout signals showed that the existing feature roadmap exceeded the team’s sustainable capacity. I had not acted on those signals early enough.

Decision: I paused new feature work for a quarter and prioritized uptime, alert quality, incident reduction, and recovery. I communicated the delayed commitments and operating risk to partners and helped hire three engineers within three months. We monitored change failure rate to guide improvements to release safety, and used playbooks and workflow automation to reduce repetitive operational work and speed recovery. I accepted a few additional minutes of lead time for changes to incorporate code-based quality and security monitoring into the delivery workflow.

Outcome: The platform completed FYE2023 with zero financially impacting incidents. As these practices matured, monitoring change failure rate helped us maintain a 99.95% availability SLA and meet a 99.99% SLO, with observed uptime averaging 99.999%—five nines. By my final year, I had reduced paging to an average of four pages per week. Playbooks and workflow automation brought mean time to recovery (MTTR) for toil-related incidents below five minutes. Adding a few minutes to lead time for changes through quality and security monitoring reduced deployment rework and improved security.

The SLA was the availability commitment, the SLO was the service objective, and five-nines uptime was the observed result. The recovery figure applies to toil-related incidents. Read the reliability decision →

DORA’s delivery measures made the tradeoff explicit: a few more minutes of lead time for changes supported stronger quality and security controls, while monitoring change failure rate informed work to improve reliability and reduce deployment rework.

How tracking platform users changed the roadmap

I used adoption telemetry, support demand, and tenant conversations to understand where streaming teams were blocked and where investment would help. Tenant organizations, individual users, and running workloads each supplied a different view of platform use.

Turning onboarding friction into adoption

Problem: Support tickets, onboarding drop-off, and tenant interviews showed teams stalling at provisioning, deployment, and compliance. Those signals made the blockers visible beyond aggregate workload growth.

Decision: I sequenced self-service provisioning, golden-path templates, managed ArgoCD, tenant-level metrics, and HIPAA/PII support around those needs. Earlier launch work paired deployment templates with phased capabilities to reduce manual onboarding work.

Outcome: In 2024, annual customer growth reached at least 76% and workload growth reached 52%, with more than 500 active users. Earlier onboarding improvements eliminated approximately 80 hours of weekly toil. Tenants could provision an environment in approximately five minutes.

The active-user count, customer growth, and workload growth describe different measures. Five-minute provisioning covers one step of the onboarding journey. Read the platform adoption story →

Using tenant inventory to change connector economics

Problem: Licensed connector costs threatened continued Kafka Connect growth. A migration decision needed evidence of which connectors tenants actually used and which capabilities a replacement had to preserve.

Decision: I combined inventory, Prometheus data, and custom JavaScript analysis to identify connector use, affected tenants, feature-parity needs, and migration scope. I authored the executive alternatives analysis and helped scope an approximately $500,000 engagement with Lenses for 10 open-source connectors.

Outcome: All 10 connectors were delivered, migration reached 100% in December 2024, and the program produced close to $3 million in net annual licensing reduction. Separately, one measured year showed production tenants growing from 47 to 66 and deployed connector workloads from 823 to 1,932; the tenant figures excluded the largest tenant.

The connector deployment counts describe the installed workload base, rather than the frequency of software releases. Read the inventory-led investment decision →

Restoring tenant trust in cost data

Problem: Frequently restarted Spark executors were billed as though they ran for a full hour. The defect undermined tenant confidence in the cost signals needed to evaluate workload efficiency.

Decision: I identified the defect, worked with Product to define a prorated billing model, and influenced the external chargeback team to prioritize the correction.

Outcome: After delivery in FYE2026, one major pipeline team resumed profiling and reduced node-pool cost by approximately 30%, roughly $5 million in pipeline-specific savings. This result was separate from platform bin-packing savings and migration-related cost avoidance.

Cross-layer incident leadership

Production events could originate in a streaming job, connector, Kubernetes node, persistent disk, secrets-management dependency, cloud maintenance event, upgrade, or shared control plane. I worked from impact toward cause: establish the incident window, find the first abnormal signal, correlate Kubernetes events and application logs, test competing hypotheses, coordinate recovery, and build a unified RCA timeline.

The objective was not merely to publish an RCA. Repeated problems needed to produce a better alert, recovery automation, runbook, upgrade control, clearer RACI, or accountable product-backlog item.

SRE management perspective

The cost to operate includes infrastructure, reliability, human, customer, and change costs. Metrics, logs, traces, and profiles provide the evidence; runbooks, automation, architecture, staffing, and prioritization are the response. My approach is to observe the complete system, connect technical signals to customer and financial impact, restore service decisively, and make every repeated operational problem cheaper to detect, diagnose, and prevent.
